# The Pessimal Caper — Act II Working Outline

Chapters 8–23 (16 chapters × ~1,667 words = ~26,672 words).  

**Act III:** Deliberately not outlined yet. Discover and rethink it during drafting.

## Working approach

Act I (Chapters 1–7) plays as a straight parody of a hard-boiled detective story. Act II gradually reveals that the Hotel Beauchamp is simultaneously hosting a 1940s noir, radio sitcoms, gangster conventions, horror serials, and a masked-vigilante adventure. Everyone plays their own genre sincerely. These chapters are daily writing prompts, not an airtight structure; move, expand, or discard scenes in revision.

Jack Chase narrates retrospectively in first person, including events he reconstructs from other people's accounts. The necklace theft is the detective-story spine; the increasingly strange hotel supplies the digressions.

## Chapter-by-chapter outline

### 8 — The Crime Scene

Jack examines the substituted necklace. Charles Montana is baffled, Sally suspicious, and Peyton Halston-Kensington theatrically outraged. Jack complains about forged-jewelry mysteries despite having suggested this exact substitution method. Reggie helps reconstruct access to the jewels.

### 9 — The Usual Suspects

Jack questions baseball celebrity Slugger Slattery and his old adversary Luk See, both of whom overheard his earlier theft theories. Introduce a menacing, Wilmer-style hired gunsel who warns Jack off the case. Jack observes: **“I knew he'd be trouble in two chapters.”** The gunsel's employer is undecided.

### 10 — The Other Conspiracy / First Harmless Zombie

Sally investigates fellow debutantes' whispering and secret arrangements, mistaking a surprise entertainment plan for a jewelry conspiracy. Meanwhile Reggie and Jack encounter a clearly zombified musician carrying an instrument and heading toward rehearsal. The musician ignores them. Establish **before the gunsel's payoff** that these zombies are apparently harmless. Optional: a spectacular overstuffed-closet mishap.

### 11 — A Professional Warning

Exactly two chapters after his introduction, the gunsel corners Jack to deliver another threat. A zombie trombonist passes. The gunsel shoots him, discovers that he continues toward rehearsal, and flees in terror. Jack is unimpressed: the zombie was just going to work. The gunsel's tough-guy credibility collapses.

### 12 — The Convention

Jack investigates the visiting Japanese criminal families, including Luk See. Possible business disputes, a salesman hawking prosthetic pinkies, and an Abbott-and-Costello-style misunderstanding involving names and Slugger's baseball terminology. Luk has something to hide. Keep the comedy centered on confusion and character, not ethnic caricature.

### 13 — The Harmless Dead

More musicians take smoke breaks at the basement loading dock and return altered, still clutching instruments and heading back to Bellamy's rehearsal. Reggie notices the pattern. Bellamy refuses Wetherby's demands to vacate the ballroom, apparently out of artistic arrogance. Jack grows overconfident about the zombies' harmlessness.

### 14 — The False Necklace

Sally realizes the necklace being displayed is a forgery, but deliberately pretends it's genuine so the thieves won't realize she knows. Jack's inquiries become tangled with her independent investigation. More eccentric hotel guests and radio-comedy misunderstandings can fill the chapter.

### 15 — The Harmonica Test

Jack and Reggie encounter their **first zombie without an instrument**—a guest or employee rather than a contracted musician. Everyone panics. Jack insists zombies are harmless; Reggie points out the difference. Jack tosses it a harmonica. The zombie ignores it and attacks. **Turning point: zombies move from comic background to real danger.** The instruments are not magic; the musicians' commitment to performing explains their behavior.

### 16 — The Basement Laboratory

Reggie and Jack investigate the strange apparatus near the loading dock. The scientist is trying to **resurrect his dead dog**, not deliberately manufacture zombies. Radiation from his experiment has been zombifying musicians who slip downstairs for cigarettes, and may now affect guests and staff. His domestic, earnest motive contrasts with the consequences. How the dog experiment ends remains open.

### 17 — The Missing Entertainer

Sally follows the debutantes' secret preparations, increasingly convinced they're connected to the theft. In fact they are arranging a surprise appearance by **Dennis Day**. They have heard music through the ballroom doors and assume the singer is rehearsing with the band. Optional Burns-and-Allen-style circular witness interview. Jack may find a clue amid the confusion.

### 18 — The Maestro's Secret

Reveal that **Maestro Bellamy knows the big band is zombified**. He's been conducting nearly continuously, sleep-deprived, because **the contracted musicians keep performing as long as he keeps them playing; if the music stops, they become dangerous**. He also needs his pay: his wife has kicked him out and he faces the street if the engagement collapses. His Chapter 5 refusal to surrender the ballroom is reinterpreted as desperate containment, not mere vanity. The unspoken absurd rule: zombie musicians honor their contracts; zombified guests and employees do not.

### 19 — The Wrong Man / The Apparition

A clue appears to implicate Slugger; Jack pursues him through the hotel. Introduce the hotel's masked vigilante **late**, and immediately marginalize him. Everyone knows he is the valet; he denies it even in costume:

> “I'm not your valet, I'm **The Apparition!**”  
>
> “Well then who's going to park that Rolls Phantom that just pulled up?”  
>
> “OH, wait ... be right back.”

He can reappear later if useful, but needs no elaborate independent superhero plot.

### 20 — The Man Zombies Fear

Reggie observes zombies backing away from Luk See, and a roughly **ten-foot safe radius** around him. Characters speculate about his hat, umbrella, criminal reputation, etc. The real reason is the **genuine necklace**, which Luk carries after the theft. Reggie starts testing the proximity effect.

### 21 — The Client's Betrayal

Jack discovers Peyton hired Luk to steal the necklace using **Jack's own suggested substitution scheme**. She insisted Luk retain the original so she could blame him if the forgery was discovered. Peyton's outraged-relative performance is exposed. Jack tries to recover the necklace while preserving some semblance of a detective story.

### 22 — The Scientific Explanation

Jack and Reggie connect the basement experiment, radiation, zombification, and the necklace's protective effect. The necklace shields a roughly ten-foot area against the effect and repels existing zombies, though the exact physical explanation needn't be nailed down in November. The scientist's dog-resurrection project has become a hotel-wide crisis.

### 23 — Everything Explained, Nothing Solved

The debutantes' conspiracy is a surprise singer; Peyton is the thief behind Luk; the orchestra is undead; Bellamy has been keeping it playing to prevent attacks; the scientist's dog experiment is the source; the necklace offers protection. Wetherby needs the ballroom for the ball and guests are assembling. **Most subplot mysteries are revealed, but their consequences remain unresolved.** Stop here: no premature Act III blueprint.

Act 2 should end with Pierce roughing up Jack to get him off the case.  Jack is tied up in a basement next to an old grandfather clock and gets rescued by Sally ... the opposite of the nancy drew plot he'd imagined.

## Continuity and running-gag notes

- **Zombie progression:** harmless instrument-carrying musician (Ch. 10) → gunsel humiliates himself (Ch. 11) → repeated apparently harmless musicians (Ch. 13) → instrumentless zombie attacks and harmonica fails (Ch. 15) → Bellamy's contract-and-conducting predicament explained (Ch. 18).
- **Bellamy's secret:** His Chapter 5 tantrum over ballroom access retroactively becomes an act of desperate containment. Musicians remain professional after death; guests and employees become dangerous. This is an **unspoken** rule, not an exposition speech about zombie labor law.
- **The Apparition:** Delay his entrance until Ch. 19; deflate him with the Rolls-Royce Phantom parking request immediately. His identity is obvious to everyone except, apparently, himself.
- **Necklace:** Sally's late mother's sentimental necklace is a lesser lookalike of a far more valuable historic piece. Peyton mistakenly believes she's stealing the famous one. **Charles's explanation that she stole the wrong necklace is reserved for late Act III**, not revealed here. The lesser necklace is nevertheless the one with the zombie-repelling property.
- **Hotel Beauchamp continuity:** In *Pessimal Hero* and *Pessimal Design*, the later-abandoned hotel is discussed with the matter-of-fact exchange: “I heard it was haunted.” “No, not ghosts. Zombies.” *The Pessimal Caper* tells the origin of that running punchline. No need to call attention to it in the novel.
- **Narration:** Jack's hard-boiled retrospective voice can digress into absurd previous cases, including *The Mystery of the Haunted Delicatessen*, and recount scenes he did not personally witness.
- **Optional radio/cinema textures:** Fibber McGee closet avalanche; Burns-and-Allen circular conversation; Gildersleeve-like pompous official; Abbott-and-Costello wordplay; Captain Spaulding reference; cab-driver informant; Charles cheerfully mistaking zombies for normal guests; prosthetic-pinky salesman; Wetherby's managerial exasperation.
- **Tone in drafting:** Act I is recognizably detective parody. Act II gradually reveals the radio dial of overlapping genres. Don't police tonal excess or scene length until draft two.

## Unresolved on purpose

Who hired the gunsel; what Slugger's real complication is; the exact timing of each scientist/zombie discovery; how much the scientist understands; whether the dog returns; whether the vigilante matters again; how the protective necklace works; and **all of Act III**. These are discoveries for the writing process, not prerequisites for November.
