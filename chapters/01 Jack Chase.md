# Jack Chase

It was a cold, dreary Wednesday two years ago.  It had rained all day, and the city smelled like dead fish and wet feet.  The humidity hung thick in the air somewhere between a New Orleans roux and full-blown etouffee. I had already polished off two cases that day, along with a pretty good bottle of rye I bought after a nice old lady paid me to find her cat. I was ready to leave, ready to slink out to the corner and start the serious drinking, when I saw a shadow at the door.  When it creaked open, the light behind it slowly shaped that ominous shadow into an even more dangerous form: an hourglass figure in a pencil skirt and a veiled cartwheel hat, slinking into my office.

I knew intuitively I was in trouble.  Her legs were far too long for the frame they were supporting, her hair was the color of that crud-covered cinnamon hard candy at the bottom of your grandmother's purse, and her lips were slathered in something waxy and shiny that matched the color a bit too perfectly. Her coat was made from some animal that probably thought it was more dangerous than she was, but her attitude dared anyone to ask what kind of fur it was.  I would have bet money she’d killed it herself.

As a private dick, I had three rules about whom I do business with: never take a dying sea captain with a knife in his back, a Zulu chieftain, or redheads in trouble on as a client. The first two invariably bring trouble right behind them. The latter will persuade me to go looking for trouble under every rock and boop it on the nose when I find it.  Heck, if the dame sheds tears, I’d probably do it for free, in the nude.  Fortunately, this was no ordinary redhead, and I was fairly sure not a Zulu chieftain this time. That bought me a few precious minutes to prepare my resolve.  I wasn’t falling for her lines, her story, or her money, no matter how badly she needs my help.  I was playing it cool, and aloof this time.

“Mr. Chase?”

“That's what it says on the door.”

She stepped into the light a few seconds after her perfume did, smelling like wealth and self-assurance.  The smoky eyeshadow only reinforced her motif. It looked like it was smeared on from the ashes of one sucker's dreams and another’s youthful idealism that she had rolled up and smoked in front of them both out of spite. I was cool.  I was aloof. I was … intrigued, now.

“I … I need your help,” she said in a way that instantly made me reach for the .45 in the top desk drawer. I didn’t, though. I was cool. I was aloof.  I was a responsible gun owner.

“A lot of people come in here needing my help.  Then a lot of people are ultimately disappointed.”

She slowly unsnapped a clutch bag and pulled out three crisp twenties.  I heard at least a dozen more left behind, silently screaming “pick me, no, pick me,” as she did. She held them up between two slender fingers, her impeccable nail polish matching her hair and her lips.  I nodded noncommittally.  I was cool. I was aloof. I was starting to drool on my desk a little.

“My name is Peyton Halston-Kensington,” she continued. It was a statement, but she phrased it like a question, as if the name should mean something to me.  Well, maybe it did, and maybe it didn't, but I know better than to start blabbing what I know and don't know in front of clients and people I’m recounting my stories to.  I'm not a jerk.

I pretended to be unimpressed, grabbed a cigarette from the box on the corner of the desk, and lit it all in one smooth motion. It was my coolest, most aloof move. It completely failed to impress her.

“My niece, Sally Montana, is having her coming out this weekend at a debutante gala.”

Now my hackles were up. “I have rules about taking cases involving debutante balls, banjos, or ventriloquist dummies,” I said dismissively. If she questioned my rules, then I could throw her out on the street as an uncooperative client. The only reason I hadn't yet was that she was dangling three months’ rent in front of my face. The last thing I needed was to be trapped at a debutante ball.  The debutante ball is what happens when a rich couple discovers they’ve spent eighteen years raising a daughter and decide the only sensible thing to do is invite four hundred strangers over to inspect her. I'd done that dance one too many times. It never pans out the way you hope, believe me. “Sorry, not interested,” I said, blowing smoke toward the door.

She extracted a fourth bill from her bag.  My jaw dropped and I was definitely slobbering.  She sighed, bored with my display of tough guy machismo. Obviously, she loved me for my mind.  “You see, Sally will be coming into her inheritance, a necklace, a family heirloom called ‘The Green Destiny.’  It's rarely brought out in public, and well, my brother-in-law, Sally's father, is a bit of an imbecile. He’s being far to boisterous about it. Now, I'm almost certain someone is going to try to steal it before her big moment.  You understand, as her aunt, I can't allow anyone to ruin that.”

I wasn’t paying attention to a word she said.  Like the sap she took me for, I just saw the money suspended between her well-manicured fingers.  My guts told me this dame didn't give a damn what happened to her niece or how stupid her brother-in-law looked having the family jewels heisted in public. Instead, I just shrugged and did my best impression of disinterested.

“Pass,” I said politely to Andrew Jackson, who was dangling inches off my nose.

The dame didn't slow the pitch for a minute. “I just thought, well, maybe if someone like you were there, well, then surely nobody would try anything unwholesome and spoil my niece's big weekend. Surely, you see my meaning.”

I didn't.  “What's the dingus worth, roughly?”

“Oh, I'm sure I have no idea,” she said in a tone that sounded like it absolutely kept every receipt. “I mean, it's not even a particularly attractive piece of jewelry,  just a big emerald in the middle of a gaudy gold necklace, really.  I  mean, certainly there are legends surrounding it, but …”

Oddly enough, cursed family jewelry wasn’t on any of my lists, though in hindsight, it should have been.  I know I should have had my secretary, Marjorie, lock the door when she went home for the night, but the problem was, the most lucrative cases, and the most desperate clients, always arrived after hours, when you were their last hope. Sure, that was in her favor. Sure, I should have gone with my gut, turned down the easy money, and gone home. Thing was, the case made me the detective I am today, and gave me a half-dozen new things to put on my list of clients and cases to avoid. So, naturally, I sat there as a dog doing tricks for four days’ work paid in advance.

“Fine. Tell me about this ball,” I said, snatching the money with my right hand and a notepad with my left. She smiled and straightened, trying to act as if spending eighty bucks on a man’s dignity was somehow “cute” to her.  After a brief stare-down where I was pretty sure she was the cat, and I was the mouse, I pulled another cigarette from the box, handed it to her, and lit it.  The trick still didn’t impress her.  Tough dame.

“It's three nights from now at the Hotel Beauchamp, in their Cobalt Ballroom.  It’s been six months in the making. Mothers threatening caterers and procuring flower arrangements, just to let their daughters drink ginger ale and dance with a bunch of lecherous firstborn sons whose greatest accomplishment is that their grandfathers owned banks and probably laundered money for the mob during Prohibition.”

“Right, I’ve been to a few. I know the drill. What's the ask?”

“My niece and her father will be arriving in the city tomorrow and staying at the hotel to help set up the ball.  He'll bring the jewels, which he plans to keep in the hotel safe until they are unveiled at the ball on Saturday night.  I want you there to case the joint, scope out the most obvious suspects, and foil any attempts to steal the Green Destiny. I believe I’ve met your fee for those three days.  Consider the fourth a paid day off on me should you be successful.”

“I’m sure the Beauchamp has a competent hotel detective.  Why don't you just use him?”

“Well, for one thing, this requires a certain degree of secrecy and discretion.  I can't have people knowing whom I'm watching and whom I am associating with. Nobody must find out we even know each other, much less see us together in public.”

“You sound like my ex-wife,” I said as I stubbed out my cigarette. “I’m guessing you suspect a plot, maybe even a specific someone. That’s why you’re spending all this money up front.”

She nodded. “I don’t trust anyone, and I suspect you’re the same way, Mr. Chase. That ball will be attended by twenty-three other families with equally spoiled daughters. The real point, of course, is to gain insider information, curry favor with potential clients, make business deals, perhaps marry one or more of your sons into one of the other society families and elevate your social status.”

“If that’s the case, suppose some potzer gets lucky and makes with the goods despite my best intentions?”

She considered this for a moment.  “Fine.  In addition to your rate, expenses, and a bonus day’s pay, if the Green Destiny isn’t stolen during that time, I’ll pay you a $200 bonus.  Now, I’m certain you won’t let me down.”

“On the other hand, you technically just wagered $200 that I will screw this up.” I tried not to laugh. “Now you sound like my mother. Alright.  I'll take the job. Who else knows you've hired me?”

“Just my brother-in-law and my niece.”

“Smart. Okay, where's the necklace now, and what time does it arrive?”

“It's still at my brother-in-law's house. He and my niece will check in tomorrow at 2 PM, then take Sally dress shopping. Most of the other families won’t arrive until Friday morning.  After that, the wives spend the day decorating, and the husbands play golf, or whatever it is men do when they're trying to impress each other.”

I thought through the timetable and considered a few scenarios. “Okay, the hotel is fully staffed during the day, its broad daylight, and the primary suspects haven’t even checked in yet. I’ll wager if they’re smart enough to crack a hotel safe, they’re also smart enough not to do it until the night shift.”

“So, what will you do?”

“Where are they shopping tomorrow?”

“Pinkerton's department store on 7th Avenue. Why?”

“Well, obviously, I’m gonna need to buy some new shoes for the ball.”

“You should buy a better suit as well.  Last time I saw a suit like that, they were signing a treaty at Versailles.”
