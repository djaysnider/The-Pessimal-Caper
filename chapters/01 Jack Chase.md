# Jack Chase

It was a cold, dreary Wednesday two years ago.  It had rained all day, and the city smelled like dried fish and wet feet.  The humidity hung thick in the air somewhere between a New Orleans roux and full-blown etouffee. I had already polished off two cases that day, and a pretty good bottle of rye I bought after a nice old lady paid me for finding her cat. I was ready to leave, ready to slink out to the corner and start the serious drinking, when I saw a shadow at the door.  When it creaked open, the light behind it slowly shaped that ominous shadow into an even more dangerous form: an hourglass figure in a pencil skirt and a veiled cartwheel hat, slinking into my office.

I knew intuitively she was trouble.  Her legs were far too long for the frame they were supporting, her hair was the color of that crud-covered cinnamon hard candy at the bottom of your grandmother's purse, and her lips were slathered in something waxy and shiny that matched the color a bit too perfectly. Her coat was made of some animal that probably thought it was more dangerous than she was, but her attitude dared anyone to ask what kind of fur it was.  I would have bet money she’d killed it herself.

As a private dick, I had three rules concerning potential clients: never take a case from a dying sea captain with a knife in his back, a Zulu chieftain, or redheads. I  had already learned my lesson on all three accounts, multiple times.  The problem was,  this was no ordinary redhead, and I was fairly sure not a Zulu chieftain this time. That bought her a few precious minutes to convince me to go against my instincts. She started with her perfume, which smelled like wealth and self-assuredness.  The smoky eyeshadow only reinforced her point, looking like it was smeared on from the ashes of one sucker's dreams and another’s youthful idealism that she had rolled up and smoked in front of them both out of spite. Obviously, I was ... curious.

“Mr. Chase?”

“That's what it says on the door.”

“I ... I need your help.”

“Most people do.  Then most people are ultimately disappointed.”

She slowly unsnapped a clutch bag and pulled out three crisp twenties.  I heard at least a dozen more left behind, silently screaming “pick me, no, pick me,” as she did. She held them up between two slender fingers, her impeccable nail polish matching her hair and her lips.  I nodded noncommittally, trying to keep professional decorum and not drool on my desk.

“My name is Peyton Halston-Kensington,” she continued. It was a statement, but she phrased it like a question, as if the name should mean something to me.  Well, maybe it did, and maybe it didn't, but I know better than to start blabbing what I know and don't know in front of clients and people I’m re-telling my stories to.  I'm not a jerk.

I pretended to be unimpressed, grabbed a cigarette from the box on the corner of the desk, and lit it all in one smooth motion. It was my coolest move. It failed to impress her.

“My niece, Sally Montana, is having her coming out this weekend at a debutante gala.”

Now my hackles were up. Redheads were strike one; debutante balls were a swing-and-miss strike two. If the words "ventriloquist dummy" or "banjo" came out of her mouth, I'd throw her out on the street. The only reason I hadn't yet was that she was dangling three months’ rent in front of my face. The last thing I needed was to be trapped at a debutante ball, which is what happens when a rich couple discovers they’ve spent eighteen years raising a daughter and decide the only sensible thing to do is invite four hundred strangers over to inspect her. I'd done that dance one too many times. It never pans out like you hope, believe me.

“Sorry, not interested,” I said, blowing smoke toward the door.

She extracted a fourth bill from her bag.  I wiped a bit of drool from the corner of my mouth.

She sighed, bored with my stamina.  “You see, Sally will be coming into her inheritance, a necklace, a family heirloom called ‘The Green Destiny.’  It's rarely brought out in public, and well, my brother-in-law, Sally's father, is a bit of an imbecile.  I'm almost certain someone is going to try to steal it before her big moment.  You understand, as her aunt, I can't  allow that.”

None of the warning signs were registering with me.  Like the sap she took me for, I just saw the money suspended between her well-manicured fingers.  As near as I could tell, this dame didn't appear to be the type to give a damn what happened to her niece or how stupid her brother-in-law looked having the family jewels heisted in public. Instead, I just shrugged and did my best impression of disinterested.

“Pass,” I said, staring at Andrew Jackson dangling inches from my face.

The dame didn't slow the pitch for a minute. “I just thought, well, maybe if someone like you were there, well, then surely nobody would try anything unwholesome and spoil my niece's big weekend. Surely, you see my meaning.”

I didn't.  “What's the dingus worth, roughly?”

“Oh, I'm sure I have no idea,” she said in a tone that sounded like it absolutely kept every receipt. “I mean, it's not even a particularly attractive piece of jewelry,  just a big emerald in the middle of a gaudy gold necklace, really.  I  mean, certainly there are legends surrounding it, but ...”

Oddly enough, cursed family jewelry wasn’t on my list, though in hindsight, it should have been.  I know I should have had Marjorie lock the door when she went home for the night, but the problem was, the most lucrative cases, and the most desperate clients, always arrived after hours, when you were their last hope. Sure, that was in her favor. Sure, I should have gone with my gut, turned down the easy money, and gone home.  Sure, it would have been simpler, and it would have kept me from discovering a half-dozen other things to put on my list of don’ts.  Instead, I sat there like a dog doing tricks for four days’ work paid in advance.

“Fine. Tell me about this Debutante Ball,” I said, snatching the money. She smiled and straightened, trying to act as if suddenly being eighty bucks lighter on cash was somehow “cute.”  After a brief stare-down where I was pretty sure she was the cat, and I was the mouse, I pulled another cigarette from the box, handed it to her, and lit it.  The trick still didn’t impress her.  Tough dame.

“It's three nights from now at the Hotel Beauchamp, in their skylight ballroom.  It’s been six months in the making. Mothers threatening caterers and procuring flower arrangements, just to let their daughters drink Shirley Temples and dance with a bunch of lecherous firstborn sons whose greatest accomplishment is that their grandfathers owned banks and probably laundered money for the mob during Prohibition.”

“I’ve been to a few. I know the drill. What's the ask?”

“My niece and her father will be arriving in the city tomorrow and staying at the hotel to help set up the ball before Saturday.  He'll bring the jewels, which will be kept in the hotel safe until they are unveiled at the ball.  I want you there to case the joint, scope out the most obvious suspects, and foil any attempts to steal the Green Destiny. I believe I’ve met your fee for three days.  Consider the fourth a paid day off should you be successful.”

“I’m sure the Beauchamp has a competent hotel detective.  Why don't you just use him?”

“Well, for one thing, this requires a certain degree of secrecy and discretion.  I can't have people knowing whom I'm watching and whom I am associating with. Nobody must find out we even know each other, much less see us together in public.”

“You sound like my ex-wife,” I said as I stubbed out my cigarette. “I’m guessing you suspect a plot, maybe even a specific someone. That’s why you’re spending all this money up front.”

She nodded. “That ball will be attended by twenty-three other families with equally spoiled daughters. Everyone who knows about that necklace has at least a passive interest in the political clout from embarrassing Charles Montana in a public setting, possibly even using the situation to ingratiate one of their sons into a marriage with Sally and the Montana fortune.”

“If that’s the case, suppose some potzer gets lucky and makes with the goods despite my best intentions?”

She considered this for a moment.  “Fine.  In addition to your rate, expenses, and a bonus day’s pay, if the Green Destiny isn’t stolen during that time, I’ll pay you a $200 bonus.  Now, I’m certain you won’t let me down.”

“Technically, you just wagered $200 that I will screw this up.” I tried not to laugh. “Now you sound like my mother. Alright.  I'll take the job. Who else knows you've hired me?”

“Just my brother-in-law and my niece.  I don't trust anyone else.”

“That's smart.  Okay, where's the necklace now, and what time does it arrive?”

“It's still at my brother-in-law's house.  He and my niece will check in early tomorrow afternoon. He’s planning to leave the necklace in the hotel safe, then take Sally dress shopping. Most of the families will arrive on Friday morning so the wives can decorate and the husbands can play golf, or whatever it is men do when they're trying to impress each other.”

I thought through the timetable and considered a few scenarios. “There are any number of ways to steal a necklace from a hotel safe, but broad daylight, with the full hotel staff on duty, right after it arrives and few people know it’s there, is unlikely if they’ve got any sense.  I’m pretty sure it’s safe until the night shift comes on, at least.”

“So, what will you do?”

“Where are they going shopping tomorrow?”

“Pinkerton's department store on 7th Avenue. Why?”

“I’m gonna need to buy some new shoes for the ball.”

“You should buy a better suit as well.  Last time I saw a suit like that, they were signing a treaty at Versailles.”
