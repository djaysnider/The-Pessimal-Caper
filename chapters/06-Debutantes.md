# Chapter 6. Debutantes

Sitting in the lobby since the night before, and for the entire time Reggie and I walked what I was increasingly convinced was about to become a crime scene, I had my trusted contact, Mickey "The Meter" Malone, watch Weatherby's office.  Mickey was a cab driver and had been since the Hoover administration. He claims just about every criminal in the city has ridden in his cab at one point, and some still do regularly. The difference is, now, they're Senators.

Before you go questioning me about drafting cabbies into my cases, let me explain: Every detective has three essential connections he relies on.  The first is a bartender who miraculously overhears news about everything shady going on.  It doesn't matter if it wasn't even said in his bar.  I don't know how this works; maybe there's a surveillance system, and they all compare notes on the weekends.

The next is a doctor who doesn't report gunshot wounds, will come to your office and do a quick suture on a knife wound, and who acts concerned when you put your shirt on and head right back out after minor surgery, even though they really couldn't care less, as long as your bill gets paid out of your estate.

Finally, and most importantly, is the mystical cab driver who knows where everything is, where everyone lives, and a mystical 4th dimensional shortcut between where you are and where you're going that lets you beat the bad guy there. I'm pretty sure it's part of your hack license exam.  I've known a lot of cab drivers over the years. Most of them were decent fellows, but make no mistake, you are an inconvenient interruption to an otherwise pleasant afternoon of smoking cigarettes, reading racing forms, and arguing with pedestrians. Mickey wasn't like that.

Mickey was like family.  He was always there when I needed him; he always kept my secrets, and he never pretended he knew something when he didn't.  Come to think of it, he was better than family. He never once asked me for money or invited himself over for Thanksgiving dinner.  In return, I cut him in on the action when I felt it was safe enough and interesting enough.  Mickey had a code he lived by, and that code meant staying out of other people's drama unless you have a very good reason to get involved.  In Mickey's case, a "good reason" was defined as "a saw buck and the reasonable possibility of seeing someone get punched in the mouth."

When we hit the lobby, Reggie went immediately for the concierge desk to make himself a few nickels.  I took a seat behind Malone, facing the other direction to keep things on the down-low. “Mornin’ Meter.”

“Mornin’ Chase.”

“Quiet night?”

“What the hell have you gotten yourself into with this one, Chase?”

“What do you mean?”

“There are some real mean customers mixed up in all this.”

“You spotted Luk See?”

“No, is he here?  Gezz, it’s been forever. I’d love to catch up with him.  No, I’m talking about these society moms and their henchmen.”

“Henchmen?”

“Yeah, this one over here, Miriam Henebery, threatened to have her chauffeur put the florist in cement overshoes for the carnations being two shades darker than the doilies.”

“Seems excessive.”

“No, excessive was what Josephine Seigmann told her dressmaker to do to the caterer with her pinking shears because the crumble topping on her coffee cake was too dry. These are some bad people. That redhead is the worst of the lot.”

“Halston-Kensington? That’s the client, Meter.”

“Then do yourself a favor.”

“What’s that?”

“Don’t screw up this case.”

“Hadn’t planned to.”  I slipped a ten behind my back and into his pocket.  “Same time tonight?”

“You got it. Watch your caboose.”  He got up and started for the exit.  

Weatherby entered the lobby seconds later. “Ladies, I do apologize for the interruption.  Maestro Bellamy has agreed to let you begin decorating the ballroom. However, he asked that you keep noise to a minimum and not disturb the band, as they are in a very delicate part of their rehearsals.

A smattering of approving grumbles rose from the ladies as they made their way to the elevator. I grabbed a copy of the morning paper off the table next to me and kept my eyes on the door to Weatherby’s office while he escorted the mothers to the elevators.

Sally and the debutantes got the ballroom shortly before the mothers and were shocked to see the entire north quarter of the ballroom, from the front of the stage to the freight elevators behind the stage, was now separated by a 60 -foot wall of fabric made from the blue velvet drapes that normally covered the windows on the other three sides of the ballroom, hastily sewn and tied together and hung from the chandeliers, completely obstructing the view of the stage and backstage entrance.  From behind it, a twenty-piece band played “Moonglow” with immaculate precision and competence.

All of the girls had spent the morning at an impromptu tea party held by Josephine Grapewin.  While Sally knew almost all the other girls and had actually gone to school with many of them, a small contingent of six girls from the “Upper Weaver” region kept mostly to themselves and spoke in some private code only they seemed to know.  When word came that the mothers were on their way up, and once the eye-rolling and accompanying giggles had died down, the Upper Weaver contingent disappeared to one corner, pretending to admire the late-summer view of the city below.

Sally, of course, could not bear to be on anything but good terms with anyone, and had taken it upon herself to slowly maneuver herself to that corner of the room to introduce herself properly to the group and assure them that there was no slight intended toward them or Upper Weaver in general if they felt excluded by the other girls. It was simply that they had all grown up together and gone to school together.

She stopped a few feet short, not really eavesdropping, but listening to ensure she wasn’t intruding on private business where she was otherwise unwelcome. The girls hadn't noticed her yet and continued their discussion.

The leader seemed to be a brunette she knew as Gladys Balsam, whose family built and owned a large steel mill in the mud cove area of Weaver Bay, a few miles outside the city. “We already know where the target is, and the plan has been in motion for two weeks now.  I don’t know why she’s getting cold feet now.”

“She hadn’t met the Chinaman before,” said Clara Hofbaue, a stern-looking blonde girl and heiress to a family architecture fortune whose name was on half the buildings in the city, including the Hotel Beauchamp.

“What difference does that make? We paid his fee. He’ll do as we ask,” Gladys argued.

Hilda Laskey, the rosy, strawberry-haired daughter of a prominent real-estate magnate, shook her head. “We don’t want anyone hurt.  He definitely looks like the type of person who would hurt someone.”

“He’s a professional. We told him where the target was. We told him the best time to make the snatch. We gave him the time and location of the drop-off.  He’ll do as he’s told if he wants the second half of his money.”

Pricilla Moray buried her hands in chestnut curls and shook her head. “Great, but what if our demands aren’t met and someone involves the police? We’re cooked. What do we do then?!”

The sixth girl, a tall, skinny, sandy-haired girl Sally knew as Margaret Polinsky, caught sight of her, slapped Pricilla on the arm to shut her up, and smiled broadly.  “Hello!  We didn’t see you there.  Sorry,” she said sweetly.

Sally was ghost-white by then and started walking backward.  “No, it’s completely my fault.  I just wondered if any of you wanted to help set up streamers.  I can see you’re busy, though.  I didn’t mean to bother you.  I’ll find someone else. Go ahead with whatever you were talking about … I mean … if you were talking … I have no idea what you were doing here.  As I said, I just wandered up.  I wasn’t really paying attention to anything.  Well, okay, we’ll talk later then.  Bye.”

Now, of course, I know all this because ten minutes later, Reggie handed me a note from Sally detailing it all and telling me to stay alert and keep watching the jewels.  She was going to investigate the girls, the Chinaman, the drop-off point, and the so-called timetable.

Aww, cripes. I should have spotted this the moment I laid eyes on the kid. She's another one of those reckless girls who thinks she's Nancy friggin' Drew. Ugh. Now, I've never actually crossed paths with Drew, but if I ever did, I'd seriously love to take this girl to task for claiming to be a detective.

For one thing, she's only eighteen ... though I think this is probably a ruse. She's been in the business for at least ten years now.  Hell, it's probably the only reason she's still alive. During that time, she's apparently faced smugglers, criminal conspiracies, and forgery rings. I also heard there may have been a kidnapper in there somewhere.  The point is, as far as I know, the worst thing that's ever happened to her is she got clocked on the head a few times, nearly drowned -- though this was an act of nature and not the bad guys -- and got tied up and left to starve in the basement of an old house once.  I mean, sure, these guys usually have soft spots for young girls, or girls pretending to be young, but it seems to me if I broke up a smuggling ring, I'd be carved into bite-sized portions and fed to their dogs.  The ones she meets helpfully explain their plans, lock her in a closet, and she gets rescued an hour later by a friend with a convenient flashlight and hairpin, and the criminal enterprise is suddenly insolvent by dinner time.

Is this girl even licensed? I am assuming not, since she doesn't charge anybody and presumably picks up her own expenses, too. That's where stuff starts adding up, too. Forget office rent, a license I have to keep current, and the occasional bartender who gets fixated on collecting a long-standing tab.  Do you know how many bad sandwiches and bottles of rye I've had to consume in my car while the person I'm tailing sits in his apartment doing absolutely nothing interesting? It starts to add up. Must be nice to have a rich lawyer father who pays your bills and buys you ice cream for making his job easier.  My old man was apparently kidnapped by a pair of cocktail waitresses 35 years ago and hasn’t been seen since, according to my mother.

So, fine, let little Sally Montana have her fun and solve the case.  Just once, I'd like to find an important clue in an abandoned mansion, secret passage, or under a mysterious staircase.  The real world doesn't work like that, though, and I was pretty sure that, by the end of the weekend, I'd either be bailing Sally Montana out of jail for felony obstruction of justice or untying her from a chair next to an old grandfather clock somewhere.
