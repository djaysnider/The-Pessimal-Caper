# The Return of the Gunsel

Reggie and Sally made their way from the lounge back to the main lobby area while I took the back exit out of the lounge, through the kitchen, pantry, and back to the loading dock that seemed to be ground zero for everything. In the lobby, Carson was frantically pacing the floor and was visibly relieved when he saw the kid.

“O'Grady! O'Grady" he called. Reggie pretended not to hear him and made a beeline for the front desk with Sally. “O'Grady, I'm calling you!”

Reggie finally stopped at the counter and began rummaging through the cabinet for the flashlight. “Oh, sorry Mr. Carson. I was deep in thought.”

“O'Grady, I told you I need you sharp, and you assured me you could handle it.”

“I know, Mr.Carson. I am, I promise. Just looking for a flashlight for our gest here.” He gestured to Sally, whom Carson seemed to notice for the first time.

“Why a flashlight?”

“Because a lamp would be very awkward to carry, Mr. Carson.”

“No, no, I mean ... nevermind. Now, 1412 called, they didn't get maid service today and want fresh towels. 819 ordered room service and it arrived already eaten. I need to run another dinner up there while I figure out who ran the last one and fire them. Finally, the gentlemen in the second floor banquet hall have had a truck, full of equipment arriving within the hour and have requested a few strong young men with carts to help them move some of the heavier stuff upstairs. Erm, you'll have to do. See if you can scrounge up anyone else.”

“Yes, sir, Mr. Carson,” he said and led Sally over toward the opposite end of the lobby. They made it halfway before hearing the familiar voice of Darla Montgomery.

“Oh, Reggie!”

Again, he tried to pretend he couldn’t hear her and made a break for the elevator. Sally grabbed him by one suspender and steered him toward the girl.

“Reggie, darling, a word please.”

“Oh, hi, Darcy. Sorry, I didn't hear you back there over the noise of the ... uhm ... elevators.”

“What? Oh, you silly boy. I've been looking for you. You said you'd show me the rooftop observatory. The one with a view of the entire city all around. I just happened to think, my parents went off to bed early, so, you know, they're asleep and won't miss me.”

“I can't right now, Darcy, I've got towels to stock and dinners to deliver, and a lot of heavy equipment to move around. Maybe some other night.”

“Now, checked. You got off at 7. What’s the matter? Don’t you want to show me that observatory?”

“I do, well, I would have, but Eugene is sick, so I have to pull his evening shift, then try to get enough sleep that I am awake for my shift tomorrow.”

Darcy sidled up and put a hand on what would have been a bicep on just about every other kid his age. “Oh, and here I thought you kept that on because you heard I couldn’t resist a man in uniform,” she purred. “Are you sure you can't take a break for a few minutes and show me a good time?”

Behind him, Sally cleared her throat.

Reggie was sweating now. “Oh, Darcy, I believe you know Sally Montana.”

“Oh, you’re Sally Montana?” Darcy asked, visibly unimpressed.

“Yes, I was wondering if--”

“You know, our fathers know each other.”

“Yes, well, everyone at these things---"

“My father talks about you a lot.”

“Oh, really?”

“Yes, I guess I just thought you’d be taller.” She turned her nose up. “I haven’t forgotten our date, Reggie. I’ll collect you later.”

Reggie watched Darcy walk back to the elevator, which miraculously opened the minute she got there.

“She doesn’t like me, but she’s crazy about you. Reggie, I need you to work on her for me.”

“Oh, come on, Sally.”

“I’m serious. You need to get in there and use your masculine charms to get her to help us.”

“How?”

“I don’t know. Do whatever comes naturally when a boy like you gets together with a girl like her, then ask her for a favor.”

“She said she was going to collect me. I don’t want to be in a collection.”

“You won’t be. If she’s anything like her grandfather, she’s no collector. She’ll simply toss you aside once she’s done with you.”

“Wait a minute---”

“I’ve got to check out that boiler room. Thanks for the flashlight, Reggie. You’re the best!” She gave him a quick peck on the cheek and started for the stairwell.

Along the way, she thought about how poorly Darcy had treated her. As if she was somehow better than everyone else just because her grandfather designed this hotel, along with most of the city’s most famous buildings, while her father was just a children’s dentist.

“You know, our fathers know each other,” she said, mimicking Darcy’s continental brogue. “My father talks about you all the time. I guess I thought you’d be taller.” She pushed the accent further into parody and worked on her facial expressions, completely failing to notice the elevator doors opening as she passed them. “I’m Darcy Montgomery, and I collect people.”

“You’re Darcy Montgomery?!” a voice came from the elevator. Sally turned and found herself staring directly at one of the Upper Weaver girls. “I’m Clara Hofbauer,” she said excitedly, extending her hand.

Sally shook it timidly. Did this girl really think she was Darcy? “Uhm, yes. Of the breakfast cereal. Our fathers know each other.”

“I know, I know. I’ve heard so much about you. You’re even prettier than I imagined, though I thought you had dark hair. Oh, never mind, it doesn’t matter. I’m so excited to meet you.” The girl's voice pitched higher after every sentence. Sally was grateful she finally stopped to breathe.

“Likewise,” Sally said timidly.

“You live in Gooseland Hills, right?”

“That’s right.”

“We live in Upper Weaver. That makes us practically neighbors!”

“Small world, eh?”

“It really is.” Clara leaned in and lowered her voice conspiratorially. “Listen. Some of the other girls and I are hatching a scheme. You know, something to show the city girls they aren’t ‘all that.’”

“Really!?” Sally asked, almost not believing her luck.

“Yeah, come on up. I’ll introduce you to the rest of the girls.”

Forgetting the basement for a moment, Sally followed her back into the elevator.

Meanwhile, outside, it had started to rain. I stepped down off the elevator and rounded the corner. Sure enough, standing in the alley, were two saxophone players, zombies of course, enjoying their evening cigarette, barely keeping it dry under the narrow eave. They both looked at me, vacantly, and made no aggressive moves. I sidled up near them, but not too near, still wary of the green glowing goo.

I pulled one out of my own pack, lit it, and held it up. “So, I hear 20,679 Physicians Say Luckies are Less Irritating. You guys agree?”

No response. They looked at me for a moment, then went back to smoking. Well, they weren’t shunning me or trying to eat my brain, at least.

“Listen, I don’t suppose either of you have seen a city waterworks guy stumbling around out here? He’s ... well, is there a delicate way to put this? He’s like you two, only...” I caught the first hint of wet, rotting corpse, “... fresher.”

They still said nothing.

The rain had already ruined my cigarette after two drags, and I was getting fed up with the conversation going nowhere. “You guys are a real barrel of laughs. We really need to do this again sometime.” I tossed the butt in the stream of glowing goo, pulled up the collar of my coat, and started off around the corner, cursing the rain the entire time.

The back side of the hotel jutted up against a narrow street, on the other side of which was an office building with a particularly good Italian restaurant on the ground floor that made the best bread. They were closed at that hour, of course, as most sensible businesses were. I made my way past the dumpsters, checking quickly for any sign of a corpse, dead or otherwise, but saw nothing. I was just approaching the west corner of the building when I caught the tell-tale scent. I instantly went on alert, pulling my trusted .45 ACP out of my jacket pocket.

“I’d know that pastrami anywhere, Gunsel. Why don’t you come on out?”

I suddenly felt the barrel of a gun poking straight into my lower spine. “Told you this wasn’t over, Chase.”

“And I told you what would happen if you showed your face around here again.”

“Technically, you said, ‘in the hotel.’ Well, we aren’t in the hotel right now. No witnesses, and no fresh-faced bellhop or smart-mouthed dame to save you this time.”

I turned to face him head-on. He grinned mischievously, revolver in his right hand, violin case in the other. “Doesn’t matter, Penny. If anything happens to me, the order goes out just the same.”

“I thought you’d say that, Chase, that’s why I had a word with my father.”

“This the part where you tell me he’s in the Hungarian mafia or something?”

Penny was taken aback. “What!? Chase, you know Pops, he’s an honest grocer from Cleveland. No, we just agreed that the exposure would be great for business, not to mention providing a good entry point to the lucrative corporate catering market. So, we loaded up the van with a complete feast, including our best meats, hoping you’d make good on that little threat.”

About this time, I noticed a shadow creeping out of the shadows behind a dumpster up the street. A vaguely man-shaped form with an unmistakable gait was approaching Penny from behind.

“Stick to sandwiches and small-time racketeering, Penny. Mészáros isn’t ready for high society. Those dames will eat you alive.”

“You haven’t tried the lox yet, Chase. You have no idea what we’re capable of.”

“I’m sure it’s great. It seems to be drawing a crowd at least.” I gestured behind him where a zombie dressed in workers overalls and carrying a pipe wrench was now a half-dozen paces away.

Penny, to his credit, didn’t freak out at all. He simply fired six shots straight into the creatures abdomen. “Is that a flippin’ zombie?!” He yelled as if I'd somehow managed to summon the undead and this was all my fault.

“Yeah, it’s a zombie, Penny. By the way, shooting them doesn’t work. Whatever organ you hit was probably half decayed anyway. You’re just making him grumpy.”

“What the hell does he want?”

“He wants you to get in your van, go home, and not show your face around here again. I thought we’d made this clear.” I began taking steps backward now. The zombie was still slow, but the wrench gave him a long reach if he decided to start swinging. I started circling, trying to lead him back to the loading dock and the service elevator once Penny was gone.

The only problem was, Penny wasn’t leaving. He’d dropped the spent revolver, opened the violin case, and was hastily trying to put the shoulder stock on his Tommy gun. “Come on, Penny, you don’t have time for that, and it won’t do any good anyway. Just run.”

“Shut up, Chase, you’ll get yours next.”

Now, I’m no hero, I don’t particularly like Penny, but I did have a soft spot for his old man’s pastrami and would hate for anything to happen that might get me banned from the deli. So, like a sap, I stood there still trying to distract the Zombie, who was more determined than ever to get at Penny. Finally, when he was within striking distance, and Penny was still fixated on getting the magazine snapped into place, I had to move in.

I awoke an hour later, face down in a puddle, with a knot on my head where the wrench hit me. There was no sign of either Penny or the plumber.
