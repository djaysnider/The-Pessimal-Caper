# The Usual Suspects

There wasn’t much time, the ball was in just over 24 hours, and I had less time than that to save Sally’s big day and my $200 bucks.  By my estimates, we had at least a dozen suspects who were in the vicinity of the bell boy’s stand the previous afternoon. Not wanting to waste any time, I immediately cornered the paper boy who works the lobby and would have seen just about everybody who came in and out over the course of the day.  I pulled two quarters out of my waist pocket and tried to approach him quietly in between rushes of people.

“EXTRA! EXTRA! READ ALL ABOUT IT! WALTERS LEADS CINCINNATI TO EVEN THE SERIES IN A 5-2 VICTORY.”

“Say, kid, can I ask you a few questions?”

“DEPENDS. ARE YOU GONNA BOOT ME THOSE ROUGHS IN YOUR HAND, JEFF?”

“It’s, Jack, actually.  Jack Chase. Can you keep your voice down for a minute?”

“NEIGHO, POPS. I’M SLAVIN’ FOR FEWS AND TWO HERE. IF I GET QUIET, THE MONEY DRIES UP.”

I tossed him a quarter, and leaned in.  “Yesterday afternoon, you were here in the lobby right?”

“SOLID, POPS. HAD THE WHOLE ROOM CORNER SEWED UP. HEY NOW, I KNOW YOU.”

“Keep your voice down, kid.”

“WHAT?”

“I said keep your voice down.”

“OH, SURE, POPS! DON'T WANNA BLOW YOUR COVER!”

Half the lobby turned to look at us.  “Exactly,” I said.

“COME OFF IT, EVERYBODY KNOWS YOU’RE THE DICK THAT QUEEN HIRED.”

“Queen?”  

“SHE’S A REAL HUMMER.”

“She’s not royalty?”

“NAH, SHE’S IN THE GROOVE.”

“She’s in a groove?”

“YOU'RE ONE SQUARE SNATCHER, JEFF.”

“I've been called worse by better people, so don’t waste your breath.  On second thought, that’s not much of a loss either.” I held up the other quarter. “Did you notice anybody paying particular attention to us when we were talking yesterday?”

“HARD TO SAY.  PRETTY MUCH EVERYBODY WAS LAMPIN’ THAT FINE DINNER.”

“It was closer to lunch.”  I handed him the quarter, then added, “Speak normal,” before I let go of it.

“THERE WAS HALF A DOZEN OF THE HOTEL STAFF AND A COUPLE OF THEM CONVENTION GUYS OVER THERE.” He pointed toward the concierge desk.  Close enough to where we were to be of concern. “THEN OVER BY YOU THERE WAS THAT BEVERLY HILLS COUPLE. THEY WERE SITTIN’ ACROSS FROM SLUGGER SLATTERY. THEN THERE WAS THAT ICKY CAT WITH THE NICE FRONT ON YOUR LEFT DUKE.”

“English, son.”

“NAH, NOT ENGLISH. PRETTY SURE HE WAS A CHINAMAN.”

“Purple suit?  Wide-brimmed hat?”

“THAT’S THE CAT.”

“Luk See.”

“SEE WHAT?”

“Nevermind.  He’s Japanese. Is that everyone?”

“THERE WAS ONE OTHER GUY. REAL YARD DOG IN A WINKLED VINE.  I THINK HE JUST JUMPED INTO PORT.”

“He jumped into what?”

“PORT. TOWN. YOU HEP?”

“I'm asking the questions, son.”

“DON'T BLOW YOUR WIG, JEFF. CAT WAS SALTY, SEE? HAD A SQUEAK BOX AND A SKY PIECE LIKE A BUSTED LAMPSHADE.”

By then, I was sure the kid was just leading me on, hoping to get another quarter.  The kid did promise to come find me if he heard anything about stolen jewelry. I could only hope he didn’t.

The “Beverly Hills” couple he referenced were Joe and Lacey Barnes. They were big-time philanthropists looking to get a new theater built in the city.  Not the type of people who would steal jewels from a little girl’s debutante ball, but then those Beverly Hills types can get a little strange at times.

I spotted them at the hotel bar. He smoked a cigar and nursed a martini.  She was sitting next to him, staring off into the distance, hands clutching her bag in her lap.

“Excuse me, Mr. and Mrs. Barnes.  Name’s Chase.  I wondered if I might talk to Mrs. Barnes for a few minutes.”

Joe Barnes rolled his eyes and took a puff off his cigar. “And you should never give up on that hope,” he said.  Then added with a sideways grin toward his wife, “I wonder why they always want to ask you questions but never me.”

Lacey simply looked at him. “Because you always have that expression on your face that says you won’t know the right answers anyway.”  She turned and extended a hand to me. “I’d be delighted to talk to you.  I’ll start. What do you do, Mr. Chase?”

“I’m a private detective.”

“Well, not anymore, obviously.”

“Not anymore?”

“Well, no, you just told us,” she said, as if it made all the sense in the world. I looked at Joe for support. He smiled knowingly. “I'm sorry if I blew your cover, but you should really be more careful. I’ll tell you what, why don't you decide what we're going to talk about?”

“I was hoping to discuss what you saw in the lobby yesterday evening around 5 PM.”

“Oh, that's a good one.  I'll pick that too.”

Her husband grinned widely and blinked at me as if understanding exactly where my brain had just gone.

“You were in the lobby yesterday at that time, correct?”

“Yes, I was waiting for a cab.”

This was surprising. “They have a cab station outside.”

“Yes, but it usually such a long wait for a cab and the seats are much more comfortable in the lobby.”

“I’m interested in learning who all was there at the time.”

“Well, I remember you were by the bellhop stand talking to that lovely lady with the big  hat.  Then there was Slugger Slattery on the couch across from you.  I talked to him for a few minutes. He's very nice. I have no idea why people dislike him so much.”

“He's lonely?”

“Yes, he said he played baseball with the Orioles for ten years.”

“That's true.”

“Well, usually people play baseball with other baseball players, but I guess for some reason they didn't like him. I can't imagine why.”

“What else did you discuss?”

“Oh, how intelligent and well-trained the seafood in Baltimore is.”

“Intelligent … seafood?”

“Yes, apparently. He says the Blue Crab there make an amazing spinach artichoke dip. Who would have guessed they could be trained to cook?”

“Can you remember anybody else.”

“Yes, there was that fella in the purple suit who hid behind the column.”

“Luk see,” I offered.

“See what?”

“His name.  He’s Japanese.”

“Oh, I don’t think so. I’m pretty sure he’s American.”

“What makes you say that."

“We’re in America.”

“I believe he can still be Japanese.”

“Well, no, then that would mean we’re in Japan. I’m sure I could tell the difference."

I decided I should try to play along and beat the woman at her own game. “Okay, but there are a lot of people speaking Chinese in the hotel as well. If they were Americans don’t you think they’d be speaking English?”

“That would make them Englishmen.”

“Ah, but we speak English, and we’re not Englishmen; otherwise we’d be in England.”

“Of course not, don’t be silly,” she said with genuine annoyance.

“So far, I remember everyone you’ve mentioned. Is there anyone else you remember? Maybe someone who looked out of place, or was acting strangely?

She thought for a moment. “Well, there was a man who kept looking at his watch. Every few minutes he’d glance at it and then sigh. I thought that was rather unkind. Watches work so hard keeping time, and there he was, staring at it as if it had done something wrong. I leaned over and told the watch it was doing a lovely job. The man looked at me as if I were the suspicious one. Which I found very suspicious of him.”

“That might be important, can you describe him?”

“Well, he had a wide-brimmed black hat, a red scarf, and a face.  The face was between the hat and scarf where you would expect it to be.”

“Was he one of the Chinamen?”

“No, he was American.”

“Yes, but did he look like … nevermind.”

“He told the concierge he was from Cleveland.”

“Cleveland?”  I wrote that down. The first useful thing I’d heard, and it probably wasn’t all that useful. “What else do you remember about him?”

“He kept walking past the elevators without getting on. He’d look at the doors, then walk away, then come back and look again. I finally asked him if the elevators had offended him. He said he was waiting for someone. I said, “Well, they won’t come out if you keep scaring the elevators like that.” He left shortly after. I believe the elevators were relieved.

“Okay, that’s good Mrs. Barnes.  What else can you remember”

“Well, he had a violin case. I asked him if he was going to play. He said no, he was going to the third floor. I said that was a shame, because the third floor could use a little music. He clutched the case tighter and hurried away. I do hope the violin wasn’t feeling rejected. Violins are terribly sensitive.”

“I’m sure they are,” I reassured her. “Anything else you remember?”

“It’s all a bit fuzzy. Almost everyone was going somewhere. Upstairs, downstairs, out the front door, into the dining room. No one seemed content to just be in the lobby. Except me. I was perfectly happy sitting there watching people refuse to stay. It’s like watching a parade that forgot the marching part. Oh, and the potted palm near the sofa kept leaning slightly to the left. I straightened it twice. The second time it leaned again, so I whispered that if it continued I would have to report it for loitering. It behaved after that. Plants can be very responsive if you speak to them firmly.”

We were now diving into insanity, and the word count has met it’s minimim for this chapter, so I decided to cut the pair loose. “I… see. Thank you for your time, Miss Barnes.”

“You’re welcome,” she said with great enthusiasm. “And if you ever need someone to watch a lobby again, just say the word. I’m excellent at noticing things that other people walk right past—like lonely watches, nervous elevators, and potted palms with poor posture.”

“I will remember that.”

Between Lacie Barnes and Lenny, the paper boy, I had learned almost nothing of value and lost what little patience I still had for trying.  It was pushing five o’clock now and I still hadn’t heard from Reggie with the list of names in his register.  I went back to the lobby and collapsed on the couch next to the bellhop stand.

As luck would have it, five minutes later, Luk See stepped off the elevator, headed toward the front entrance.  I quickly moved to put myself in the path between him and the door.

“Luk See,” I said calmly, making sure to stare into his eyes.

“Jack Chase,” he said calmly, stopping inches away from me. “Hear you’re lookin’ for somethin’.”

“Did you also hear you’re top of my list of suspects?”

“That so?”

“It’s so,” I replied, grateful I had finally found someone who spoke the same language I did.

“Wasn’t me, Chase.”

“That’s exactly what a guilty person would say. What’s a Yakuza hitman doing playing fetch for the Chinese Tongs, anyway?”

“Yakuza?!  I just did a couple of jobs for them when they had trouble getting visas.  I’m not even Japanese.”

“So, you always worked for the Tongs?”

“I’m a freelancer, Chase. I’ve worked for everybody over the years.  The Yakuza, the Genovese, the Bonannos, the Chicago Outfit, Dillinger, The Mills Brothers, all the way back to the Irish Mafia.”

“You were in the Irish Mafia?”

“I’m from Dublin, Chase.”

“With a name like Luk See? Pull the other one.”

“My real name is Luke Campbell.  Get it ? ‘Lucky’ Luke C? Lucky C?  Luk C?”

“You’re Irish?!”

“You can’t tell from the accent?”

“Honestly, these are the most words you’ve said to me in seven years.”

“Exactly, if I stole something you were looking for, would I be sticking around the hotel talking to you?  Come to think of it? Why am I talking to you at all?” He pushed me to one side and headed for the doors.

“What do the Tongs want with those jewels, Luk?”

“Why don’t you ask them?” He said without turning around.

“Because I’m asking you.”

“Why would I know? I’m not working for them,” he said as he passed through the revolving doors and out into the street.
