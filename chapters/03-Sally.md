# CHAPTER 3.  (SALLY MONTANA)

Like any red-blooded American male, I hate shopping. Always have. I've been shot four times, stabbed three, poisoned once, and hit in the head with a sap so many times that I’ve got a permanent indentation in my skull.  Nothing, however, gets my blood boiling faster than the idea of spending an afternoon in a department store with a woman who won’t know what she wants until she's seen everything she doesn't.

I’m a man.  I go to the store because I need something.  If I need multiple things, I make a list.  I buy what's on the list, check it off, pay, and leave.  A woman, on the other hand, goes into a store because she “needs something,” finds it, decides she doesn't like it, finds something else, decides she liked the first thing better, then buys a third thing because the salesgirl said it was fashionable. By the time she's finished, the first thing has gone out of fashion, the third thing has sold out, and the salesgirl has retired to Florida.

Pinkerton’s, as near as I can figure, is part of a conspiracy between clothing manufacturers and mirror owners. Every year some secret committee must meet in their stockroom, see what didn’t sell as well as expected, and then put together a show featuring those clothes, declaring whatever you bout last year was wrong, and an affront to civilization. This year, apparently the message is that skirts are too long, hats are too small, lapels are too wide, and every woman in America needs to dress like a lampshade somebody's grandmother won in a raffle.

I spotted the Montanas in the corner salon, where girl after girl walked paraded in and out of the room in every sort of gown imaginable. At the same time, little Sally looked absolutely enthralled, and her father, Charles, grew paler with each subsequent price tag reveal.  I stayed close by, hoping to get a chance to talk to both of them outside of the hotel, where nobody would see us together.

As I stood there, in the middle of the men’s department, at least four times a salesman with a tape measure around his neck walked up to me and shook his head as if what I was wearing had personally offended his family.  I don’t understand the mentality.  I’ve owned this suit for twelve years.  It’s a good suit.  The pockets are always right where I left them, the buttons function as expected, and there are no bullet or knife holes in it, making it “lucky.”

Back in the salon, Sally had apparently picked the dress and had now moved on to the hat.  I don’t know why this is such a huge deal.  As far as I’m concerned, if it hides a bad haircut, keeps rain off your face, and can be easily removed without screwing the top of your head off, then it’s doing its job.  Sally, meanwhile, was trying on hats with all manner of features and functions.  Feathers, flowers, fruit, even fowl.

“Ah, sir,” another salesman behind me said. “I couldn’t help but notice you are obviously in need of a suit. I’ve taken the liberty of pulling out a few selections.

I turned slowly and fixed him with a stare. “What makes you think I need a suit?”

The man smiled and began making nervous gestures with his hands. “Well, sir … I mean … obviously … surely something a bit more in season … would be?”

“In season?” I adjusted my cuffs and pretended to look offended. “This is all weather.”

“That much is obvious, yes.” He shrugged in half apology.

“Look, if it will make you happy, you can sell me a pair of socks.  Do you have any that are in season?”

“With apologies, hosiery is on the second floor, past the accessories, next to the ties, across the aisle from the cologne.  I am sure there is a salesman there who can—”

“I’m not getting anywhere near socks until someone has tried to sell me a belt, wallet, tie, and bottle of aftershave, am I?”

The man merely smiled.

“I hate shopping.”

Sally had apparently picked out the hat for the evening and had moved on to shoes. Now I knew something about shoes from two ex-wives, who would spend endless hours picking out shoes that were just the right style for that year, just the right color to match or complement exactly one outfit in her closet that she never wore but swore she would now that she had the perfect shoes to go with it.  Inevitably, she wouldn’t, and when pressed on why we spent enough money on shoes to buy a new car, just to resurrect an outfit she still hadn't worn, she would begrudgingly admit it was because the shoes hurt her feet.  Sally at least appeared more sensible than that.

I waited until they exited the salon, Sally beaming with pride, her father looking like he'd just realized he had made a Faustian bargain when he promised his little girl anything she wanted.

“Mr. Montana, name’s Jack Chase.  Your sister-in-law Peyton hired me.”

“Yes, of course.  She said she was going to.”  He gave the bundles to the clerk, held out a hand, and motioned for Sally to go on ahead.

“Sorry to corner you like this, but I didn’t think we should be seen talking to each other at the hotel.”

“Whatever you think is best, Mr. Chase.”  I pulled my trusty notepad and pencil out of my pocket.

“The necklace.  It’s in the hotel safe, now?”

“It is. I watched Mr. Weatherby put it in there.”

“Was anybody else there?”

“Yes, there was the hotel detective, a Mr. Pierce.”

My heart dropped to my shoes. “Not Alastair Pierce.”

“I believe it was, yes.  Do you know him?”

“I got him started in the hotel detective business.”

“Well, he should be happy to be working with you again then.”

“When I got him kicked off the police force for being a dirty cop.”

“Ah, yes, I can see where that would rather complicate things.”

He wasn’t wrong.  Pierce was a brawler known for beating confessions out of suspects and persuading witnesses they saw exactly what he needed them to see.  Not the kind of person I’d expect to be investigating which guests stole a bath towel.

“When are you getting the necklace from the hotel safe?”

“I had planned for 4 o’clock Saturday afternoon, while Sally is dressing for the gala.”

“Nice dress?”

“I'm told it's Cerulean.”

“What’s that?”

“A rather expensive shade of blue, from what I gather.  These salespeople seem to speak in their own language.”

“What’s the actual value of the necklace?”

“Oh, I’m sure I don’t know.  It’s very old and has been in my family for generations. There are … legends … but I’m sure you aren’t the type to believe in those.”

“You’d be surprised at some of the stuff I’ve seen.”  I thought for a minute.  “Do you have any particular enemies?  People you might have crossed at some point who might be looking for payback.”

Montana chuckled. “Mr. Chase, I’m a dentist -- mostly I treat children.  While I can imagine some children might hold a grudge over having a brace put on or a tooth extracted, I assure you I have no enemies of note.”

“What about Peyton? She obviously suspects something or someone, enough to hire me.”

“Well, I suspect Peyton does have quite several people who dislike her.”

“I wouldn’t put it past half the society dames.”

“I would say half is being generous.  Those are 23 of the most well-to-do suburban housewives in the city, and Peyton is, shall we say, a social climber.”  He smirked. “Well, of course, you’ve met her.”

“I’ve met her.”  I stroked the stubble on my chin, trying to find an angle for dealing with her going forward.

“Daddy,” Sally came bounding up the aisle from behind a rack of clothes. “Can we stop at the confectionery on the way out?  I had a dream about fudge last night.”

“Of course we can, Princess.  Just remember, though, you still need to be able to fit in that dress on Saturday.”

“Yes, Daddy.”

Striking up conversations with 18-year-olds in front of their parents was never my strong suit on a good day. “Congratulations on your coming out. You must be very excited for the ball.”

She shrugged. “I suppose so. I think it’s really for the mothers and my aunt, though.  I never really understood having a ball to enter a society I’ve been living in for eighteen years.  Still, if I get to hang out in the city and go shopping with Daddy, it’s worth being paraded around in front of all those cute boys.”  She kissed her father on the cheek.  “And my dress is absolutely beautiful, thank you, Daddy.”

“Anything for my Princess.”  He beamed for just a minute. “Your mother would be so proud of you.  She used to talk about this day.”

A cloud seemed to settle over the ladies' wear department.

“I do miss her.  Especially on a weekend like this one,” Sally said.

“We all do, Princess. At least your aunt Peyton is going to be there to see you.”

Sally forced a smile with no actual joy onto her face. “I suppose so.”

I filed that little reaction away in the back of my head to revisit later.

“Alright, I’ll leave you two to the fudge. Remember, if we bump into each other at the hotel, you don't know me.  If you see anything suspicious – someone taking an interest in the necklace or asking a lot of strange questions – put it in a note and have Sally here leave it at the front desk for me. Above all, remember, if you suspect someone, don’t try to handle it yourself.  Leave the rough stuff to me.”

“Fair enough, sir,” Montana smiled and nodded. “What will you do in the meantime?”

“In the meantime, I’m going to try to find a suspect for a crime that hasn’t even been committed yet and try to figure out how they’re going to pull it off before they do.”

“Seems frightfully difficult.”

“So is whistling 'Sugar Foot Stomp' with a mouth full of crackers, but I know a few tricks.”
