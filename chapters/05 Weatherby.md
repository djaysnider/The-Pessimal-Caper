I met back up with Reggie at 8 AM, Friday morning, just as Horace Weatherby arrived to find his hotel had finally descended into complete chaos. Silas Carson, the night manager, met him at his office door.  “Sir, I'm so sorry. There was nothing I could do.  They simply showed up at 5 AM and started unloading everything right there in the middle of the lobby.”
“Carson! Slow down!” Weatherby snapped. “What have we talked about.  Take big problems, break them down into smaller problems, and organize them.”
“Sorry, sir,” Carson bowed deferentially and took a deep breath. “Angry Mothers. Argumentative Maestro. Ballroom. Band. Castration. Caterers. Coffee. Debutante. Decorations. Donuts.”  He paused, considering what he'd left out. “Éclairs.”
“Okay, stop at ‘E’!” Weatherby ran through the list in his head.  “Wait ... what about the coffee?”
“The debutante mothers have already drunk everything but the Sanka, sir.”
“Dammit! Get Bellamy down here.”
Carson continued to bow as he backed out of the manager's office, only to be replaced by an imposing figure in a grey pinstripe suit and derby. 
“Ah, Mr. Pierce, good morning,” Weatherby said without looking up. “I trust you got my memo from yesterday.”
“You got a tip that one of the little brats’ mothers hired a private dick who’s been harassing employees and guests. Yes, I read it, Mr. Weatherby.”
“Good, now I don't need to tell you how bad it makes us look when our own patrons don't trust our discretion and ability to settle in-house matters ourselves. We need to discourage this kind of behavior.”
“Yes, Mr. Weatherby.”
“But we need to do it discreetly this time.”
“Discreetly, Mr. Weatherby.”
“No, you do understand what ‘discreetly' means, don’t you?  I had the impression you were confused by the word last time.”
“By any means necessary,” the big man nodded enthusiastically.
“No, it means ‘quietly,’ ‘with secrecy,’ possibly even, if I dared hope, ‘without any of our guests knowing it even happened.’”
“Oh, okay.   I can do that, Mr. Weatherby.”
“Are you quite certain,  Mr.  Pierce? Because I've seen no indication of this in your performance so far. When you found that stray rat in the storage room, you did publicly interrogate it.”
“Yes, and that was wrong.  You were right to explain that to me at the time, Mr. Weatherby.”
“So, you won't be tying rats to chairs until they reveal the names of their accomplices?”
“I will not, Mr. Weatherby.”
“And certainly not in the lobby in front of the other guests.  There really was very little chance one of them was secretly colluding, so really, you did frighten more people than you intimidated.”
“Definitely not in the lobby, Mr. Weatherby.”
“Good.  Then that will be all, Mr.  Pierce.”
“Thank you, Mr. Weatherby.”
Pierce exited the room, only to be replaced by Carson a few seconds later. “Mr. Weatherby, I have Maestro Bellamy here to see you.”
“Very good, Carson, send  him in.”
Carson took three steps backward and practically shoved Bellamy through the doorway.
“Mr. Weatherby, I really must protest. These interruptions to our rehearsal time are most intolerable.  I am an artist! I must be allowed to work!”
Weatherby buried his face in his hands. “You're an artist.  The cake decorator is an artist.  The little old lady who designed the crepe paper flowers is an artist. The servers who fold the napkins into swans are artists. The florist who arranged all the centerpieces for the tables is an artist.  The bartender who puts the fruit on the toothpicks and garnishes the drinks is an artist.”  He grew more and more unhinged the longer he spoke.  “I am up to my elbows in artists. Do you know the difference between you and the cake decorator, the little old lady who designed the crepe paper flowers, the servers who fold the napkins into swans, the florist who arranges the centerpieces for the tables and the bartender who puts the fruit on the toothpicks and garnishes the drinks, Bellamy?”
Bellamy had to admit he did not.
“The difference, sir, is that you have had access to the ballroom for thirty-six hours, and the others, as of yet, have not.”
“Mr. Weatherby, you contracted with the union for us to play a gala ball tomorrow night for some very influential families. I have assembled the orchestra, researched and selected music, and am currently exploring the acoustic possibilities of a ballroom designed, in only the roughest, most general terms, to accommodate an orchestra of this size and composition. The contract specified that I will have access to said ballroom for the three days before the morning of the event to maximize the acoustic potential of--"
“Bellamy! The contract does not specify ‘exclusive' access.”
“It was implied, sir.  Surely you understand that additional bodies moving through the room, along with the din of conversation and other gear being moved about, seriously undermines my ability to properly assess room acoustics. How can I perfectly balance the dark, woody timbre of the clarinets against the deep, rich tone of the trombones if someone changes the color of the tablecloths and moves a centerpiece three inches to the left?  No, sir, my professional credibility is on the line, Mr. Weatherby. My very career and membership in the union hinge upon successful completion of the contract I signed.”
“Bellamy.” Weatherby placed both hands firmly on the desk in front of him, which suddenly made Bellamy glad there wasn't a pointy letter opener in sight. “You know the type of people you will be playing for, correct?”
“Of course, Mr. Weatherby.  Twenty-four of the wealthiest, most influential families in the city.”
“That is correct.  So, you understand the power they wield and the kind of things they are capable of, don't you, Mr. Bellamy?”
“I do, Mr. Weatherby.”
“Well, then understand, those people's wives are currently in my lobby, abusing the catering staff, the bartenders, and the little old ladies who make crepe paper flowers while waiting for access to the ballroom to begin decorating it for the ball. Not to mention, Mr. Bellamy, that they have already drunk all the good coffee and we are down to the cheap stuff.”
Bellamy scrunched his face up. “I understand, Mr. Weatherby.”
“Now, of course, I would never ask you to break a contracted arrangement simply to make my paying guests happy, even if they are much more important than you. Likewise, I would never expect you to undermine your fine skills in acoustic architecture or compromise the subtleties of your performance. However, if you do not work out some sort of compromise in the next 30 minutes, I am going to use every power at my disposal to make sure the only gigs you and your orchestra ever play again involve cotton candy, the pier, and unattended children with very sticky fingers that insist on touching everything.  Are we clear, Maestro?”
“I will see what I can do, Mr. Weatherby,” Bellamy said as he quickly backed out of the room and tore across the lobby, nearly colliding with the kid and me.
“The Maestro’s really worked up about something this morning,” Reggie said.
“He’s not the problem kid.  The problem is: if the jewel thief gets the goods out of Weatherby’s office, how does he plan to get them through the lobby and out the door without raising suspicion?”
“He makes a clandestine handoff to someone in the lobby, then leads your men on a wild goose chase?” The kid looks hopeful, probably dreaming he’s a natural at this kind of work.
“Only in the movies, kid.  In real life, as soon as he does that, his friend points the original thief out to the doorman on his way out.  The thief gets arrested, and he never sees his ‘friend’ or the jewels again.  How many street exits does the hotel have?”
“Six: main doors, valet exit, parking garage, two fire exits, and the loading dock.”
“Okay, then, the main door has a doorman; the valet exit, valets; the parking garage, witnesses; the fire exits, alarms. So, who monitors the loading dock?”
“Whoever is using it at the time.  Porters, cleaning staff, cooking staff, the office managers. If there’s not a load coming in or going out, then it's usually deserted.”
“But always open?”
“Yeah.  I’ve seen band members sneaking away to go down there to take smoke breaks.”
“You sure they’re band members?”
“Yeah, they usually take their instruments with them.  One of the trumpet players lost his mute down there yesterday, but I found it for him.”
“Let’s take a look at those docks, kid.”
Reggie took me down a service hallway, past an old freight elevator to the loading dock behind the hotel.  Sure enough, it was just a short distance from the kitchen, the laundry room, and the main hallway---close to everything, but carefully tucked out of sight so nobody would think twice about it.  Best of all, it was completely dark, aside from a strange blue-green glow.
“What’s that stuff?” Reggie asked.
I knelt down and examined the puddle, careful not to touch anything.  “Phosphorescence. Some kind of chemical runoff,” I said. We traced the thin stream from the puddle back around the corner of the dock to a leaky pipe sticking out of the ground at the base of the building.  Whatever it was glowed much brighter back here at the source.  “Where does this pipe come from?  What’s under this part of the hotel?”
Reggie thought for a moment.  “There’s a basement.  It’s mostly just used for junk storage.  Old, outdated furniture, art, furnishings like that.  Oh, and the boiler is down there.  I can show you where---”
“No!”
“Are you sure?”
I jerked a thumb at the glowing blue goo. “Positive.”
“So, wait a minute.  You’re a big, tough detective with a gun who takes on underworld criminals, but you’re afraid of basements?”
“I’m not afraid of basements, kid.  Don’t start spreadin’ stories like that.”
“Okay, sorry, Mr.Chase.”
“It’s creepy basements I have a problem with.  Yours is definitely creepy.”
“Mr. Chase, I’ve been down there plenty of times. It’s not that creepy.”
“Oh, yeah?  See those windows?  They have bars on them.”  I gestured to a row of semi-circular windows about nine inches tall at ground level. 
“That’s to keep people out.”
“How can you be so sure it’s not to keep something in?”
“I don’t think---”
“Tell me, does this basement of yours have one bulb over the staircase that mysteriously goes out when you pass under it?  Is the handrail just a little bit loose?  Does the third step from the bottom wobble and bow as if it might collapse under the weight of someone exactly my size?”
“Well, kinda---”
“Does it have a smell?  No, the usual dusty, musty smell of a basement, but one of, I don’t know, maybe a dead former manager someone forgot to bury?”
“Now, I am almost certain---”
“Does it have noises?  Not just the furnace or the boiler making gurgling and hissing noises, but more like a former manager moaning and screaming because his corpse has been laid out in the middle of a giant pagan resurrection symbol made up of broken Christmas ornaments and cleaning supplies?”
“I’m pretty sure Pagans don’t actually---”
“Forget it, kid.  There are two kinds of detectives in this world.  Those who wander into creepy basements, and those who live long enough to write their memoirs, and I intend to be in the second camp.”
