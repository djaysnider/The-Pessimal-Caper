# Unusual Suspects

It was the dinner rush, and I still had very little to go on.  A stranger with a violin case and wide-brimmed hat, a former Yakuza assassin who, by his own admission, was working, but not for the Chinese Tong, and at least by his story, not after the necklace, which was already stolen anyway.  If Peyton was right, one of the mothers was certainly behind the whole thing, but given the window in which the crime could have been perpetrated, they were all preoccupied with decorated or otherwise tormenting the hotel staff.  Unless, of course, that was an intentional distraction while a hired expert thief did the job.

I cornered Reggie in the back hall near the service elevator which he was loading with another cart of bags from the new arrivals. “Kid, I need a favor.”

He snapped to attention faster than a boot sailor on his first trip to an Amsterdam brothel. “Yes, Mr. Chase?”

“That conductor fellow upstairs with all the debutantes and their hens?  He was acting a bit strangely this morning, wouldn’t you say?”

“Maestro Bellamy?  I suppose you could say that.  Of course, he may just be tired. That band’s been rehearsing nonstop for 48 hour now.”

“That’s a bit unusual as well, isn’t it?”

“Well, maybe he just wants everything to be flawless for so many important people?”

“Or maybe he’s overheard something one of those hen mothers said that has him so spooked he won’t leave the ballroom.”

“You think he knows something, Mr. Chase?”

“Can you get me up there to talk to him while the families are all down here at dinner?”

“Sure can, Mr. Chase, step in.”  He motioned me into the crowded car, already three-quarters full with luggage.  

Moments later, we were standing in the service corridor on the 23rd floor with a breathtaking view of the city through oversized exterior windows. We stepped into the famous Cobalt Ballroom, now mostly decorated, with tables meticulously arranged, each decorated with a gaudy artificial flower arrangement and pink doily. The far end of the room had been blocked off by the long blue velvet drapes which surrounded the bandstand, suspended by the lighting truss.  From behind it, a small orchestra played an immaculate version of “Did You Ever See a Dream Walking.”

As soon as the song ended, I chimed in.  “Maestro Bellamy, could I have a word please?”

Briefly his round, bald head popped out of the crack between two of the curtains.  “No,” he said, then disappeared.  

The band began to play “The Celery Stalks at Midnight.”

I was quickly running out of patience as well as time. “Mr. Bellamy, a crime has been committed, and you are a person of interest in the case I’ve been hired to investigate.”

Nothing.  The band continued to play.  After the first verse, Bellamy’s head poked out again, the band did not stop playing.  “I had nothing to do with any crime, I assure you. Now if you don’t mind, I am sure this is a matter that can be handled after rehearsal.”

“I don’t think so, Maestro. Could I trouble you to come out here and at least face me, sir?”

He appeared to think about this for far too long, then reluctantly heparted the curtains just enough to step through them, all but his right arm, which he defiantly kept back behind the curtain.  The band continued to play.  He was obviously still directing them while talking to me.

“Any chance you could give me your undivided attention, Maestro?”

“I have a very pressing rehearsal schedule.  One that has consumed me for the past forty-eight hours, so you see, I haven’t had time to mastermind, commit, or delegate a crime.  I’ve only left this ballroom once, and that was this morning because of the arrogance of that Weatherby.  He has no idea what that interruption nearly cost me.”

“Yes, that’s a bit odd to me as well.  Your rehearsal schedule is rather aggressive, to say the least.”

“This room is notoriously difficult to acoustically tune to.  I ne ed every bit of time I can get with such a tight deadline.”

“They sound great to me.”

Bellamy snorted and looked down his nose at me.  “You are a fellow musician then, Mr. Chase?”

“Well, I’ve been known to whip out the ol’ tin sandwich on a long stakeout.”

“Ah, of course.  The harmonica is the unappreciated heart and soul of musical composition. Perhaps Johanna Bayer can make something out of its shrill intonations, but until then, let’s just leave your unqualified opinions unspoken, and the serious musical decisions to me, sir.”

“You’re seriously going to rehearse that band straight through for another 24 hours? until they’re dead on their feet when it’s time to play?”

His expression went blank for a moment.  “Wait.  What have you heard?”

“That’s what I was going to ask you, Maestro.  I think somebody said something that spooked you.  Spooked you good enough that you’re hiding up here behind a giant curtain because you’re afraid of showing your face.  So, spill it, Bellamy.  What are you hiding from?”

“Hiding? Me. I’m afraid you’ve got this all wrong.  I’m on a serious time constraint here sir, and interruptions like these aren’t helping. You don’t seem to understand I am under contract to provide the musical entertainment tomorrow night. This is a union job. Do you know what they’ll do to me if something goes wrong?”

“Maestro, I once watched a couple of teamsters fit a man with cement overshoes for being a little loose with the petty cash box. I doubt the musicians’ union compares.”

“Oh yes? Tchaikovsky didn’t poison himself, sir.”  He glared at me in an attempt to look intimidating, spat out a quick “Good day,” and disappeared behind the curtain again.  

Seconds later, the band began playing, “Whispers in the Dark.”

Reggie suddenly appeared behind me. “Gee, Mr. Chase, that didn’t seem to go very well.”

“No, it didn’t Reggie, but it did give me an idea.  You said you found a trumpet mute out back on the loading dock, right?”

“That’s right. Sometimes musicians slip out and down there for a smoke.”

“Alright, if Bellamy won’t talk, then maybe one of the members of his band knows something and will be a bit more forthcoming.  Lead the way.”
