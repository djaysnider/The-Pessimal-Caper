# CHAPTER 1. (JACK CHASE)

It was a cold, dreary Thursday two years ago.  It had rained all day, and the city smelled like dried fish and wet feet.  The humidity hung thick in the air somewhere between a New Orleans roux and full-blown etouffee. I had already polished off two cases that day, as well as solved one minor mystery for a client. I was done, ready to slink out to the corner and start the serious drinking, when I saw a shadow at the door.  When it creaked open, the light behind it slowly formed that ominous shadow into the even more dangerous form of an hourglass figure in a pencil skirt and a veiled cartwheel hat that slinked its way into my office.

I instantly knew she was trouble.  Her legs were far too long for the frame they were supporting, her hair was the color of that crud-covered cinnamon hard candy at the bottom of your grandmother's purse, and her lips were slathered in something waxy and shiny that matched the color a bit too perfectly. Her coat was made of some animal that was probably endangered and illegal to wear, but her attitude dared anyone to ask what kind of fur it was made from.  I wasn’t going to take bets on whether she’d killed it herself or not.

As a private dick, I had three rules concerning potential clients: never take a case from a dying sea captain with a knife in his back, a Zulu chieftain, or redheads. I  had learned my lesson on all three accounts, multiple times.  The problem was,  this was no ordinary redhead, and I was pretty sure not a Zulu chieftain this time.  That bought her a few precious minutes to convince me to go against my instincts. She started with her perfume, which smelled like wealth and self-assuredness.  The smoky eyeshadow only reinforced her point, looking like it was smeared on from the ashes of one sucker's dreams and another’s youthful idealism that she had rolled up and smoked in front of them both out of spite. Obviously, I was … curious.

“Mr. Chase?”

“That's what it says on the door.”

“I … I need your help.”

“Most people do.  Then most people are ultimately disappointed.”

She slowly unsnapped a clutch bag and pulled out two crisp twenties.  I heard at least a dozen more left behind, silently screaming “pick me, no, pick me,” as she did. She held them up between two slender fingers with impeccable nail polish that matched her hair and her lips.  I nodded noncommittally, trying to keep professional decorum and not drool on my desk.

“My name is Peyton Halston-Kensington,” she continued. It was a statement, but she phrased it like a question, as if the name should mean something to me.  Well, maybe it did, and maybe it didn't, but I know better than to start blabbing what I know and don't know in front of clients and people I’m re-telling my stories to.  I'm not a jerk.

I pretended to be unimpressed, grabbed a cigarette out of the box on the corner of the desk and lit it.  It was my coolest move. It failed to impress her.

“My niece, Sally Montana, is having her coming out this weekend at a debutante gala.”

Now my hackles were up. Redheads were strike one; debutante balls were a swing-and-miss strike two, right next to ventriloquist dummies and banjos. The last thing I needed was to be trapped at a debutante ball, which is what happens when a rich couple discover they’ve spent eighteen years raising a daughter and decide the only sensible thing to do is invite four hundred strangers over to inspect her. I'd done that dance one too many times. It never pans out like you hope, believe me.

“Sorry, not interested,” I said, blowing smoke toward the door.

She extracted a third bill from her bag.  I wiped a bit of drool from the corner of my mouth.

She sighed, bored with my stamina.  “You  see, Sally will be coming into her inheritance, a necklace, a family heirloom called ‘The Green Destiny.’  It's rarely brought out in public, and well, my brother, Sally's father, is a bit of an imbecile.  I'm almost certain someone is going to try to steal it before her big moment.  You understand, as her aunt, I can't  allow that.”

None of the warning signs were registering with me.  I just saw six months' rent suspended between her well-manicured fingers.  As near as I could tell, this dame didn't appear to be the type to give a damn what happened to her niece or how stupid her brother looked having a necklace stolen out from under him hours after he retrieved it from the family vault. Instead, I just shrugged and did my best impression of disinterested.

“Pass,” I said, while staring at three month’s rent dangling inches in front of my face.

The dame didn't slow the pitch for a minute. “I just thought, well, maybe if someone like you were there, well, then surely nobody would try anything unwholesome and spoil my niece's big weekend. Surely, you see my meaning.”

I didn't.  “What's the dingus worth, roughly?”

“Oh, I'm sure I have no idea,” she said in a tone that had absolutely kept the receipts. “I mean, it's not even a particularly attractive piece of jewelry,  just a big emerald in the middle of a gaudy gold necklace, really.  I  mean, certainly there are legends surrounding it, but …”

Oddly enough, cursed family jewelry wasn’t on my list, though in hindsight, it should have been.  I know I should have had Marjorie lock the door when she went home for the night, but the problem was, the most lucrative cases, and the most desperate clients, always arrived after hours, when you were their last hope still away. Yeah, she had that in her favor. Yeah, I should have gone with my gut, turned down the easy money, and gone home.  It would have been simpler, and kept a half-dozen other things off my list of don’ts ever since that weekend.

“Fine. Tell me about this Debutante Ball,” I said, snatching the money from her hand. She sniffed and straightened, trying to act as if suddenly being sixty bucks lighter on cash was no big deal.  After a brief stare down where I was pretty sure she was the cat and I was the mouse, I pulled another cigarette from my case, handed it to her, and lit it.

“It's two nights from now at the Hotel Beauchamp; their skylight ballroom.  It’s been six months in the making.  Mothers threatening caterers and procuring flower arrangements, just to let their daughters drink Shirley Temples and dance with a bunch of lecherous firstborn sons whose greatest accomplishment is that their grandfathers owned banks and probably laundered money for the mob during Prohibition.”

“I’ve been to a few. I know the drill. What's the ask?”

“My brother and niece will be arriving in the city tomorrow and staying at the hotel to help set up the ball before Saturday.  He'll bring the jewels, which will be kept in the hotel safe until they are unveiled at the ball.  I want you there to case the joint, scope out the most obvious suspects, and foil any attempts to steal the Green Destiny. I will pay your fee in advance for the three days.”

“The Beauchamp has a pretty competent hotel detective.  Why don't you just use  him?”

“Well, for one thing, this requires a certain degree of secrecy and discretion.  I can't have people knowing whom I'm watching and whom I am associating with. Nobody must find out we even know each other, much less that we are working together.”

“You sound like my ex-wife. Moreover, I’m guessing you suspect someone. That’s why you’re spending all your brother’s money up front.”

She nodded. “That ball will be attended by twenty-three other families with equally spoiled daughters. Everyone who knows about that necklace has a vested interest in relieving my niece's family of it, if not for greed, then for the political clout of embarrassing my brother Charles in a public setting.  Some may even hope to blackmail him into marrying off Sally to one of their sons as a bold power move.”

“If that’s the case, suppose some potzer gets lucky and makes with the goods despite my best intentions?”

“I’ll pay your expenses for the weekend.  If the Green Destiny isn’t stolen during that time, I’ll pay you a $200 bonus.  I’m reasonably sure you won’t let me down.”

“Technically, you’re wagering $200 that I will, though.” I stifled a laugh. “Now you sound like my mother.  Alright.  I'll take your job. Who else knows you've hired me?”

“No one, I assure you.  I don't trust anyone enough to tell them.”

“Okay, that's good start at least.  Where's the necklace now and what time does it arrive?”

“It's at my brother's house.  He and my niece will check in tomorrow afternoon to oversee final preparations.  He’s planning to leave the necklace in the hotel safe, then take Sally shoe shopping.  That would be the most likely window for a burglar to act, I would think.”

Clients always assumed they knew the business better than I did, and they usually proved it by offering helpful bits of advice like that. “There are any number of ways to steal a necklace from a hotel safe, but fewer optimal times.  Broad daylight, two days before the event, with the full hotel staff on duty? No way. Too many eyes. Too tight a window. No other suspects to pin it on.  No, it will be a last-minute heist with all of the other families there as witness for maximum effect.”

“So what will you do?”

“Where are they going shopping tomorrow?”

“Pinkerton's department store on 7th Avenue. Why?”

“I’m gonna need to buy some new shoes for the ball.”

“You should buy a better suit as well.  Last time I saw a suit like that, they were signing a treaty at Appomattox.”
