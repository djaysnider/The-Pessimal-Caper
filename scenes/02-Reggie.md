# CHAPTER 2. (REGGIE O’GRADY)

“O’Grady, Mr. Hu's trunk just arrived.  Take it up to room 504 and unpack it for him. He'll be late checking in,” the front desk clerk said, a bit more aggressively than was warranted.

“On it!” Came the eager voice of a nineteen-year-old, freckle-faced bellhop, who was already carrying a large suitcase in each hand, a hatbox under his left arm, and a cloth-covered birdcage in his teeth.  He expertly hooked a wheeled cart with his left foot and set everything on it before grabbing the trunk and manhandling it onto the end.  “504.”  He grabbed the nametag on the trunk’s handle and studied it. “Mr. Hu,” he said quietly to himself, then pointed to the larger suitcase and hatbox … “219” … and the smaller suitcase and birdcage … “624.”

“And hurry back. The convention attendees are starting to arrive.”

“Convention?!”

“Yeah, some sort of Japanese gambling thing.  Oh, and before you get back, go to the lost and found.  One of the trumpet players rehearsing in the ballroom lost his mute this morning.  See if anyone has found it and turned it in.”

“Will do, boss.”

A second bellhop, taller, lankier, and a bit older than Reggie, came around the corner.  “Need help, Reg?”

Eugene McDougal was the type of person who rarely, rarely was nice for nice's sake, and the fact that he was offering now meant that he'd decided Reg had gotten enough accolades for one day and was planning to sabotage him in advance of the convention.

“That's okay, Eugene, just three sets of luggage to deliver.  Do you want to go to the lost and found and see if there's a mute there?”

“A mute?”

“Yeah, one of the trumpet players in the ballroom lost his.”

“Why don't you do that, and I'll run the luggage up.”

Reggie thought for a minute and decided having Eugene out of his hair for the time it took to unpack the trunk would be time he couldn't spend sabotaging him.

“Yeah, fine, you can run these three drops.”

“Three drops.  Sure.  Where am I going?”

Well, the trunk is for Mr. Hu and needs to be taken to 504 and unpacked. The large bag and birdcage are for Mr. Watts in 624.  The small bag and hatbox are for Mrs. Mei in 219.”

“Okay, so whose bags go to the fifth floor?” asked Eugene.

“That's right.”

“What's right?”

“No, Watts is on six.”

Eugene blinked. “I didn't ask about six.”

“Good. Now take his bird up there.”

“Whose bird?”

“No, Hu’s is the trunk and it goes to five.  He hasn’t checked in yet.”

“Then why are we taking his trunk upstairs?”

“Because he sent it ahead.”

“Who did?”

“Exactly.”

Eugene stopped, cocked his head to one side, and inspected the items on the cart.

“Who’s the hat for?”

“Mei.”

“You?”

“No.”

“Who?”

“No. Mei.”

“What’s the name of the lady in 219?”

“No, Watt’s the guy in 624?”

“I’m not asking you who’s in 624!” Eugene demanded.

“Hu’s in 504.”

“Why are you asking me?”

“I’m not.”

Eugene took another deep breath.  “Who is in 504?”

“Not yet,” said Reggie. “But his luggage will be.”

“Whose luggage?”

“Right, the big trunk.”

“I ALREADY KNOW THE TRUNK IS GOING TO FIVE!”

“Then we're making progress.”

At this point, a small gentleman in a gray suit approached the counter and presented a claim ticket.

“Watt,” he said.

Eugene turned on him. “What?” The gentleman nodded. “What can I do for you?”

“Watt.”

“Yes, what?”

The gentleman pointed to his ticket.

Reggie accepted it. “Good afternoon, sir. We were just taking your bird up to six. Would you care for an escort?”

The gentleman smiled and followed a waiting porter toward the elevators.

Eugene stared after them.

“You knew who that was?”

“Of course.”

“How?”

“He told me.”

“He said ‘what’!”

“Yes.”

“That's not telling us anything!”

“It told me his name.”

Eugene took a long breath. It was a fine breath, full of hope and the promise of a fresh beginning.

“Reggie. Let's start again. Who is on five?”

“Mr. Hu.”

“And what's on six?”

“Mr. Watt.”

“Who is checking in?”

“Not until late.”

“WHAT?”

“No, he's already checked in. He just left us and went to his room.”

Eugene stared at Reggie like a particularly dim dog trying to read a map of bus routes. The telephone rang, and Reggie grabbed the receiver before the echo died.

“Bell desk. Yes, Maestro. Certainly, Maestro. I was just headed to lost and found.  If I see it, I'll have it sent up immediately.”

He hung up and made a note.

“What's that?” asked Eugene.

“A mute for the ballroom.”

“Who?”

“No, Mr. Hu is on five.”

“I said WHO NEEDS A MUTE?”

“Maestro Bellamy. His trumpet player.”

“Then send the trumpet player!”

“He's already in the ballroom.”

“Then why does he need to go there?”

“He doesn't. The mute does.”

A tall, thin gentleman in a battered overcoat entered the lobby. He wore a crushed hat over curly reddish-blonde hair, carried a walking stick, and had a small automobile horn tucked under one arm. He approached the desk without saying a word.

Reggie smiled.

“Good afternoon, sir. Checking in?”

The gentleman shook his head.

“Checking out?”

Another shake.

“May I help you?”

The gentleman produced the horn and gave it a short, mournful honk.

Eugene pointed triumphantly. “Oh, you found him!”

Reggie looked at the gentleman, then at Eugene. “No, Eugene. That's a gentleman.”

“He doesn't talk!”

“Perhaps he has nothing to say.”

The gentleman nodded vigorously.

“He's a mute!”

Reggie considered this. “I haven't asked.”

Eugene snatched the telephone receiver from the counter. “Ballroom, please! Tell Maestro Bellamy his mute is down here!”

The gentleman's eyes widened. He shook his head violently, seized Eugene's sleeve, and honked three times. “See?” said Eugene. “He's excited!”

“I believe he's objecting.”

“To what?”

“Being sent to the ballroom.”

“Then why did he come here?”

The gentleman produced a room key.

Reggie examined it.

“Ah. Room 814. Your bags have arrived, sir. Shall I arrange an elevator?”

The gentleman bowed, tucked his horn beneath his arm, and started away.

Eugene shouted after him. “WAIT! THE MAESTRO NEEDS YOU IN THE BALLROOM!”

The gentleman stopped, turned, and blew a raspberry through the horn.

“Temperamental musicians,” Eugene muttered.

“He isn't a musician,” said Reggie.

“How do you know?”

“He hasn't an instrument.”

“He's carrying a horn!”

“Yes, but I don't think it's in the orchestra.”

At that moment, another gentleman appeared at the counter. He wore an immaculate dark purple suit and carried an umbrella despite the bright afternoon. He had the composed, faintly dangerous expression of a man who knew several ways to end a conversation without speaking.

“Luk See,” he said.

Eugene turned.

“Where?”

“Luk See.”

“Yes, I see you. How can I help you?”

The man rolled his eyes. “My name.”

“I don't know  your name.”

The gentleman's expression did not change. Reggie consulted the register.

“Ah, Mr. Luk See. Welcome to the Beauchamp. You’re in room 912. Your party has already arrived.”

“See?” said Mr. Luk See.

“See what?” demanded Eugene.

“Luk See.”

“YES, I CAN SEE YOU!”

Mr. Luk See studied Eugene for a moment, then looked at Reggie. He handed over a claim ticket.

“Your cases are in 912, sir,” said Reggie. “Will there be anything else?”

Mr. Luk See shook his head.

“Very good, sir.”

He departed. Eugene leaned heavily against the counter. “Why does everybody tell you what they want?”

“Because I ask them.”

“I ASK THEM!”

“You tend to shout before they finish answering.”

“I DO NOT!”

“Very good, Eugene.”

Before Eugene could formulate an objection, a fourth visitor arrived, a short, round-faced gentleman with a small suitcase and an air of growing dissatisfaction.

“Wei,” he announced.

Eugene closed his eyes. “No.”

The little man smiled and nodded. “Yes.”

“Wei.”

“No, no, no.”

“Noh, yes.”

“I SAID NO!”

The gentleman frowned and pointed at himself. “Wei.”

“Your name is Wei?”

“Noh Wei.”

“Okay, then what is your name?”

“Noh Wei.”

“Sir, we can't let you check into the hotel if we don’t have your name in the register.”

“Noh.”

“No?”

“Wei.”

“WAIT!”

“No, Wei.”

Two approving honks came from the elevator.

Eugene looked at Reggie. “Is he doing this deliberately?”

“I shouldn't think so. It's his name.”

“No way!”

“Correct.”

Reggie checked the register. “Mr. Wei. Room 611. Welcome, sir.”

The gentleman bowed gratefully.

Eugene pointed at Reggie. “Wait!”

Mr. Wei shook his head. “No, Wei.”

“NO, I MEAN WAIT!”

“Wei!” the man insisted.

“YES, WAIT!”

Mr. Wei shook his head. “No.”

Eugene gripped the counter. “Look!”

“Yes?” Luk See poked his head around the corner where he was still waiting for an elevator.

“Then DON'T wait!”

Mr. Wei looked bewildered.

From across the lobby came a single honk.

The silent gentleman from 814 had returned. He was standing beside a potted palm, watching the proceedings with obvious interest.

Mr. Wei turned toward him. “Wei?”

The silent gentleman nodded and honked twice.

Mr. Wei smiled. “Wei!”

Another honk, and the two men shook hands.

Eugene could only stare. “What is happening?”

“I believe they've introduced themselves,” said Reggie.

“How?”

“Very successfully, by the look of it.”

Mr. Wei pointed toward the elevators.  “Wei?”

The silent gentleman gestured with his horn. One long honk, two short ones. Mr. Wei nodded and headed toward the lifts.

“WAIT!” cried Eugene.

Both gentlemen stopped.  Mr. Wei looked back.  “Wei!”

The silent gentleman honked.

“NO!” shouted Eugene. “I'M TALKING TO HIM, NOT YOU!”

Mr. Wei pointed at himself. “Wei?”

“YES, YOU!”

Mr. Wei shook his head and pointed at the silent gentleman.  Eugene's mouth opened. Nothing came out. The silent gentleman gave a cheerful little toot.  Reggie stepped around the counter.

“Mr. Wei, your room is on six. The gentleman with the horn is on eight. I'll take you both up and see that your luggage arrives.”

Mr. Wei beamed. The silent gentleman saluted with his walking stick.

“Eugene,” Reggie continued, “Would you go to lost and found and see if there's a trumpet mute there?  Maestro Bellamy needs it in the ballroom.”

Eugene straightened his jacket.

“Of course. I can handle that.”

“Thank you.”

Reggie led the two gentlemen toward the elevators.  Eugene watched him go then walked over to the concierge.  “You see what I have to put up with? That boy has no ambition. No initiative. He'll never get anywhere in this business.”

“Seems to be getting to the elevators,” the concierge said.

“That's not what I mean. I'm talking about advancement. Responsibility. Leadership. A man has to make himself indispensable.”

“Sure.”

“Mr. Wetherby knows he can count on me.”

“That's nice.”

“Why, just this morning he said that if anything went wrong in the lobby, he knew exactly who to blame.”

The concierge congratulated him.  A moment later, the elevator doors opened again. Reggie stepped out alone, carrying a small black object.

“What's that?” Eugene demanded.

“The trumpet mute.”

“I thought you said it was in Lost and Found!”

“It was. I collected it on the way down.”

“But I was supposed to get it!”

“You were watching the desk.”

“I could have done both!”

“I know. That's why I asked you.”

Reggie gave him an encouraging smile and headed toward the ballroom.

Eugene stared after him. “I don't understand that boy.”

“Neither do I,” the concierge admitted. “But if I ever need to move a piano through a revolving door, he's the first man I'm calling.”

From somewhere near the elevators came a triumphant blast of an automobile horn. Eugene seized the telephone. “Ballroom! Tell Maestro Bellamy we've found his mute!”
